# Mathematical Notes — Autoencoder Anomaly Detection

## 1. The autoencoder objective

Given normal transactions $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$, an autoencoder learns an
encoder $f_\theta: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^k$ ($k \ll d$) and a decoder
$g_\phi: \mathbb{R}^k \to \mathbb{R}^d$ minimizing reconstruction error on
normals:

$$\min_{\theta,\phi}\ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{normal}}}\left[\|x - g_\phi(f_\theta(x))\|_2^2\right].$$

The anomaly score of a new transaction is its reconstruction error
$s(x) = \|x - g_\phi(f_\theta(x))\|_2^2$. The decision rule is
$\hat{y} = \mathbb{1}\{s(x) \ge \tau\}$ for a threshold $\tau$.

## 2. Why reconstruction error separates anomalies: the manifold assumption

Normal transactions are assumed to concentrate near a low-dimensional
manifold $\mathcal{M} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ (here: $d=29$, bottleneck $k=4$).
The encoder–decoder pair learns an approximate projection onto
$\mathcal{M}$: for $x$ near the manifold, $g_\phi(f_\theta(x)) \approx x$ and
$s(x)$ is small. A fraud $x'$ generated by a different process typically
lies **off** the manifold, so no $k$-dimensional code reconstructs it well
and $s(x')$ is large.

Two failure modes follow directly from this picture, and both appear in
practice:
- **Frauds on the manifold** (small, ordinary-looking transactions) get low
  scores — the autoencoder is blind to them by construction.
- **Rare-but-legitimate transactions** (e.g., very large amounts) lie off the
  manifold and get high scores — false positives are structural, not a bug.

This is why the score distributions (see `results/figures/score_histograms.png`)
overlap: unsupervised detection buys label-freedom at the cost of an
irreducible error region.

## 3. Why ROC-AUC is misleading under extreme imbalance

With 492 frauds in 284,807 transactions ($\pi \approx 0.0017$), ROC-AUC can
look excellent while the detector is operationally useless. The math:

$$\text{TPR} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN}, \qquad \text{FPR} = \frac{FP}{FP+TN}.$$

FPR divides by the **huge** negative population: at FPR $= 1\%$,
$FP \approx 0.01 \times 284{,}315 \approx 2{,}843$ false alarms — nearly
$6\times$ the total number of frauds. The ROC curve barely notices, because
the denominator $FP + TN$ is dominated by $TN$.

Precision, by contrast, divides by the **alarms actually raised**:

$$\text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP}.$$

At 0.17% base rate, even a good detector's precision is modest, and the
precision–recall curve shows it honestly. **Average precision (PR-AUC)** is
therefore the primary threshold-free metric in this project; ROC-AUC is
reported only for comparison, with an explicit warning.

A useful rule of thumb: with base rate $\pi$, a detector with
$\text{recall} = r$ and $\text{FPR} = f$ has
$\text{precision} \approx r\pi / (r\pi + f)$. For $\pi = 0.0017$, holding
precision above 50% at $r = 0.8$ requires $f \lesssim 0.0014$ — a false-alarm
rate under 0.14%. Thresholds must be chosen in FPR/precision space, never by
staring at ROC-AUC.

## 4. Threshold selection as decision theory

A threshold $\tau$ trades two costs: $c_{FP}$ (analyst review of a false
alarm) and $c_{FN}$ (the monetary loss of a missed fraud). The Bayes-optimal
rule flags when

$$P(\text{fraud} \mid s) \cdot c_{FN} > P(\text{normal} \mid s) \cdot c_{FP},$$

but neither the posterior nor the costs are known precisely. The project
compares three practical strategies (all tuned on validation, evaluated on
test):

1. **precision@k**: flag the top-$k$ scores. Answers "with a review budget of
   $k$ cases, what fraction are fraud?" — operationally direct, but $k$
   presumes knowledge of the fraud count.
2. **F1-optimal**: $\arg\max_\tau F_1(\tau)$ on validation. Balances precision
   and recall, but the optimum is sensitive to the validation fraud mix and
   ignores asymmetric costs.
3. **Fixed FPR**: $\tau$ = $(1 - \alpha)$-quantile of validation normal
   scores. Directly controls review load: expected daily alerts
   $\approx \alpha \times$ daily volume. This is the recommended operating
   point — it is the only strategy whose parameter maps to a business
   constraint (analyst capacity) rather than a property of the labeled sample.

## 5. What supervision buys (the XGBoost reference)

The supervised XGBoost model sees labels and optimizes a discriminative
objective; the autoencoder sees only normals and optimizes reconstruction.
The expected ordering is

$$\text{PR-AUC}_{\text{XGB}} > \text{PR-AUC}_{\text{AE}} \gtrsim \text{PR-AUC}_{\text{IF}},$$

and the experiment confirms it (see README results). The gap between the
first two terms is the **price of label-freedom** — the quantity of interest
when labels are unavailable, delayed (chargebacks arrive weeks later), or too
scarce to train on. Reporting the supervised ceiling honestly is what makes
the unsupervised result interpretable instead of merely "worse".
